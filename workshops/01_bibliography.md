# Workshop 1: Bibliography and literature review

**Duration:** 70 min · **Tools:** an AI assistant with web search, Consensus or Elicit, PubMed, Zotero (optional)

**Goal:** use AI to find, summarise, compare and check scientific literature on a topic you teach, and end up with a bibliography you can trust.

<div class="key">

**Choose your topic** (or use your own):
A. Simulation-based training for nursing or medical students
B. Interventions to improve hand hygiene compliance in hospitals
C. Peer-assisted learning in health professions education
D. Use of large language models in medical education

</div>

## Exercise 1: The trap (15 min)

Do this exercise **first**, as a group: open the handout **“Can you trust this bibliography?”** (data/references) and check the 8 references.

What you should learn from it: a reference can look perfect and still be invented. **Checking that a DOI “works” is not enough; check *which* article it opens.**

## Exercise 2: Search with an AI that cites its sources (15 min)

In an assistant **with web search enabled** (or in Consensus / Elicit), ask:

```
I am preparing a lecture for [Year 2 nursing students] on [topic].
Find 5 to 8 key peer-reviewed publications from the last 10 years,
favouring systematic reviews and meta-analyses.
For each one, give: authors, year, title, journal, DOI or PMID,
study design, and one sentence on the main finding.
Only include publications you found in your search, with a link.
```

Then repeat the **same search on [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov)**, for example with filters *Systematic review* and *last 10 years*.

- Do the AI's results appear in PubMed?
- Did the AI miss important papers? Did it find some that PubMed did not?

## Exercise 3: Summarise and compare (20 min)

Choose 2 or 3 papers. Open them yourself (the abstract at least, the full text if it is open access), and upload or paste them into the assistant:

```
Here are [3] articles on [topic].
1. Summarise each in 5 lines for a teacher with no statistics background.
2. Compare them in a table: population, intervention, comparison,
   outcomes, main result, level of evidence, main limitations.
3. Where do they agree? Where do they disagree?
4. What would you conclude for teaching practice, and how confident
   can we be?
Base your answer only on these articles, and quote the passages
that support each claim.
```

**Check:** pick two claims from the table and find the quoted passage in the article. Is it accurate?

## Exercise 4: Critical appraisal (10 min)

```
Act as a critical peer reviewer. For [article], identify the main
risks of bias (selection, measurement, confounding, attrition,
reporting), the limits of generalisability to [our students],
and 3 questions I should ask before relying on it in my teaching.
```

The AI is a good **sparring partner** for appraisal, but the judgement is yours. Compare with a standard checklist (CASP, GRADE, or the one you usually teach).

## Exercise 5: Build the bibliography (10 min)

- **With Zotero:** open each article (DOI → publisher's page or PubMed) and save it with the Zotero browser connector. Zotero gets the metadata from the source, not from the AI. Then export it in your citation style (Vancouver, APA…).
- **Without Zotero:** ask the assistant to format the references you have **checked** in Vancouver style, then check one or two entries against PubMed.

<div class="warn">

**Golden rule:** never cite a reference you have not opened yourself. AI tools can fabricate references, attach a real DOI to the wrong paper, or misreport a study's findings.

</div>

## To go further

- **Explaining a paper to students:** “Explain this paper's design and result to Year 1 students, with an analogy, then write 3 discussion questions.”
- **Journal club:** “Prepare a 10-minute journal club presentation outline on this paper, with the key figure to show.”
- **Search strategy:** “Help me build a PubMed search strategy (MeSH terms and keywords) for [question].” Then run it yourself on PubMed.

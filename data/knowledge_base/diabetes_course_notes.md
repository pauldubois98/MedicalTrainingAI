# Course notes — Diabetes mellitus essentials (Year 2)

*St Aldric School of Health Sciences (fictional institution). Teaching summary for Year 2 students; always refer to current national guidelines in practice.*

## 1. Type 1 and type 2 diabetes

Type 1 diabetes results from the autoimmune destruction of the insulin-producing beta cells of the pancreas, and requires lifelong insulin therapy. Type 2 diabetes is characterised by insulin resistance and progressive beta-cell dysfunction; it is strongly associated with overweight, physical inactivity and family history.

## 2. Diagnosis

Diabetes can be diagnosed with an HbA1c of 48 mmol/mol (6.5%) or above, a fasting plasma glucose of 7.0 mmol/L (126 mg/dL) or above, or a random plasma glucose of 11.1 mmol/L (200 mg/dL) or above in the presence of symptoms. In the absence of symptoms, the test must be repeated to confirm the diagnosis.

## 3. First-line treatment of type 2 diabetes

Lifestyle measures (diet, physical activity, weight loss) are the foundation of treatment. When drug treatment is needed, metformin is the usual first-line medicine, unless contraindicated or not tolerated. Gastrointestinal side effects are common at the start of treatment.

## 4. Recognising hypoglycaemia

Hypoglycaemia is usually defined as a blood glucose below 4.0 mmol/L (about 70 mg/dL). Symptoms include sweating, trembling, palpitations, hunger, anxiety, confusion and, in severe cases, seizures or loss of consciousness. It is most often caused by insulin or sulfonylureas, missed meals or unplanned physical activity.

## 5. Treating hypoglycaemia

In a conscious patient who can swallow, give 15 to 20 g of fast-acting carbohydrate (for example glucose tablets or a small glass of fruit juice), then recheck the blood glucose after 10 to 15 minutes and repeat if it is still below 4.0 mmol/L. Once the level has recovered, give a longer-acting carbohydrate snack or the next meal. An unconscious patient must never be given anything by mouth: call for help, and give intramuscular glucagon or intravenous glucose according to local protocols.

## 6. Long-term complications

Chronic hyperglycaemia damages small vessels (retinopathy, nephropathy, neuropathy) and large vessels (coronary heart disease, stroke, peripheral arterial disease). Annual screening includes eye examination, kidney function and urine albumin, and foot examination.
